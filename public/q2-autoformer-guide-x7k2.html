1Why this is not a forecasting problem

Everything below was measured on the data.

  • The autocorrelation of y is 0.966 at lag 1, about 0.4 at lag 24 and about 0.03 at lag 168. A 168-step forecast built from y's own past is mostly a guess at the mean level. "Same as last week" scores 98 RMSE. An oracle that knows the true weekly mean and predicts it flat scores 80.
  • The covariates, which are known for the hidden week too, explain far more. The chart shows leave-one-year-out results.
  • Cross-correlation shows yt is most related to covariates at t−1 through t−6, and the relation fades for leads beyond about +12. So y behaves like an integral of recent conditions, which is what a sequence model should learn.

This is the TA's "state estimation" or now-casting point. His other hint, where the optional data should go, is answered in section 5.

2Autoformer in three ideas

Series decomposition

Split a sequence into a slow part (a moving average) and a fast part (what is left). Autoformer does this after every sub-layer and keeps two streams. The fast stream goes through the network. The slow, or trend, stream is accumulated and added back at the end.

In plain words

Think of "the week's overall level" and "the hour-by-hour ripples" as two separate things the model keeps apart, so neither drowns the other.

Auto-correlation

Attention asks which positions look alike. Auto-correlation asks: if I shift the sequence back by τ steps, how well does it line up with itself? It keeps the best K shifts, softmaxes their scores, and outputs a weighted sum of the shifted copies. With the FFT it costs O(L log L).

Encoder and decoder

The encoder reads a context window. The decoder produces the 168-hour output and cross-correlates with the encoder's memory.

4How the external features were examined

The manual asks which of the ten variables carry signal and which portions of the file are worth feeding to the model. The honest account has two parts. While building the model I did not search over columns. All ten stayed in, and I narrowed the representation: what to derive from them, which stretch of time to feed, and where in the network to put them. After the model was built I ran a column-by-column diagnostic to answer the per-column question directly. It is labelled as after the fact, and it did not change the model.

What I did, in order

  1. Read the types. A to F are continuous, and E and F are integer counts. G to J are four 0/1 indicators, and in all 43,824 rows exactly one of them is set. E is non-zero in 0.8% of hours and F in 4.1%.
  2. Screened pairwise correlations of log(1 + y) with each covariate at lags from −48 to +48 (table below). The strongest relations are with D and with the direction indicators, and they peak when the covariate is measured one to six hours before y. They shrink towards zero for leads of 24 to 48 hours. So y is related to the recent past of the covariates, which is why the task is estimating the current state.
  3. Looked inside D. Its increments are positive, it resets to a small value, and every reset coincides with a change in the direction indicators. I replaced it with two derived channels, the per-step rate and the steps since the last reset (section 3). Ablation: without them the 3-seed ensemble scores 62.9 instead of 58.6.
  4. Tried time representations on fast models (ridge, then an MLP) before building any Autoformer. Pointwise ridge scored 85. Trailing windows of the recent covariates brought it to 69.6, leading windows added about 0.5 and y about 0.7. An MLP on the same window features reached 59 to 61, so nonlinearity matters as well. This is why the Autoformer receives trailing windows of 3 to 168 hours.
  5. Decided where the file enters by ablation: decoder only, encoder only, or both (section 5).
  6. Checked hour-of-day marks built from the integer index (index modulo 24). They were neutral, so they are not in the base model. They are in 3 of the 12 ensemble members for diversity.

Why correlation alone would have misled me

Column B never correlates above 0.11 with anything, and a model given only B scores about 92, which is no better than guessing the average. Yet removing B from the full model costs 1.0 RMSE (MLP) to 2.2 (ridge). A column can matter only in combination with others, so I did not discard columns on a pairwise screen.

What this says about each variable

  • D (the cumulated column) is the most informative group in both models, and its derived parts carry most of that. Removing only the derived rate and spell length costs 4.5 (ridge) and 2.3 (MLP), consistent with the raw-channel ablation on the Autoformer.
  • A is the strongest column for the nonlinear model. Alone it reaches 69.9, and dropping it costs 4.1. Ridge sees much less of it (+1.1), which suggests A acts through interactions.
  • The direction indicators G to J matter (+2.8 for the MLP). Alone they reach 84, better than a flat guess but far from the full set.
  • B and F add about 1 each, and only in combination with other columns.
  • C is ambiguous: +0.9 for ridge but +0.03 for the MLP. Treat it as weak.
  • E carries essentially nothing. Dropping it changes ridge by 0.02 and the MLP by 0.34, within noise. It is non-zero in under 1% of hours.

My guess is that B matters through its relation to A, since the pair behaves like a gap between two measurements, as in a humidity measure. I did not test that.

What I did not do

  • I did not run a leave-one-column-out search inside the Autoformer itself. The diagnostic uses ridge and an MLP, and a difference of about 1 RMSE or less is within their noise.
  • I did not remove E or C from the final model. The diagnostic suggests dropping E would cost nothing, but I never retrained the Autoformer without it.
  • I did not tune the window lengths. The set {3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 168} was chosen in advance from the time scales in the cross-correlation. E and logD are not windowed (E is almost always zero, and the rate derived from D already carries its history). Neither choice was tested.

5The model, step by step

Shapes: B is the batch, L = 336 the context, LB = 48 the label, H = 168 the horizon, d = 32 for configuration A, and C is about 82 covariate channels. The code is in af.py.

What each part is allowed to see

Components

  • Embedding. A Conv1d along time (kernel 3, replicate padding) that mixes all C channels into d, so each position sees ±1 hour.
  • Decomposition. MovingAvg with kernel 25, centred and edge-replicated so the length is preserved. The trend of the hidden states is extracted after each sub-layer. In the decoder these trends are projected d → 1 and added up (the accumulated trend), while the fast stream goes on.
  • Auto-correlation. AutoCorr computes R(τ) = Σt qt kt−τ via the FFT, keeps the top K = ⌊3·ln T⌋ delays per example, applies a softmax with a learned temperature, and outputs Σj wj vt−τj. I implement the aggregation as a circular convolution with a sparse K-tap kernel. That is one FFT product, exact, and needs no gather (the first version ran out of GPU memory). A self-test checks it against a brute-force O(T²) computation.
  • Cross-correlation on a shared timeline. Encoder positions are times [o−L, o) and decoder positions are [o−LB, o+H). I place both on one timeline of length L+H, zero-padded, and normalise by the overlap count, so "lag τ" always means τ real hours. As I remember it, the reference implementation pads or truncates the two sequences to a common length without regard to absolute time. Check the paper's code before quoting that.
  • Output. The seasonal projection plus the accumulated trend, mapped to physical units with expm1.

Knowing the past weather is useless for the next week. It is the future weather inside the horizon that carries the signal, so it must enter the decoder. Adding it to the encoder as well helps a little, within noise at one seed.

How y enters, and why it mostly does not

  • y in the encoder, the standard design, made the model memorise. Training RMSE was about 45 and held-out about 70. A 120k-parameter network can recognise a training window from its 336 y-values and recall its answer. The with-y runs were mostly single seeds (70.7, 75.2, 78.0), all at 70 or above, against a 6-seed y-free mean of 64.5, so the gap is large relative to seed noise. The memorisation mechanism is my explanation of the train/held-out gap. I did not test it directly.
  • y is not thrown away. A bridge adds trh = Σj gj[h] · mean(last wj y's) with w in {1, 3, 12, 48} to the trend stream. The gain g is learned per horizon step and starts at 0, so the model starts exactly y-free. With only four scalar inputs it has no capacity to memorise. A self-test confirms that y older than 48 steps cannot reach the output.
  • Effect of the bridge: a 6-seed mean of 63.1 against 64.5 without. That is directionally positive and not significant.

Output link and loss

ŷ = expm1(z·sd + mu) is non-negative and multiplicative. It is trained with MSE in physical units, masked on interpolated rows, because that is the ranked quantity. The exp link beat a linear output on the final configuration with 3 seeds each: 61.6 against 69.7 per seed, and 58.6 against 65.6 for the ensembles.

6What I changed from the reference Autoformer

The reference is Wu et al. (AAAI 2021) and its public code. What I say about it comes from my memory of that code, and I have not re-checked it line by line, so verify before quoting any of it. Each change has a status chip. Measured next to a change means a comparison exists. Not ablated means the reason is reasoning only, and no measurement shows it helps.

Covariates, not the target, are the network's inputCompared on seeds

Reference
The encoder embeds the target series plus calendar marks. The decoder embeds the last 48 target values (the label) followed by zeros for the horizon.
This model
Both embed covariate channels only. y never enters an embedding.
Why
With y in the encoder a network of this size memorises: training RMSE about 45, held-out about 70. What matters over 168 steps is in the covariates, so the network gets those and nothing that identifies a particular training window.
Evidence
Runs with y scored 70.7, 75.2 and 78.0 (mostly single seeds) against a y-free 6-seed mean of 64.5. That memorisation is the cause is my explanation. I did not test it directly.

Covariates enter the decoder across the horizonAblated

Reference
For the horizon the decoder input is zeros plus calendar marks. Measured variables are never given for the future.
This model
The decoder embeds all covariate channels over 48 label steps and 168 horizon steps, and the encoder embeds them too.
Why
The optional file covers the hidden week, so the future values are known and are the signal. Only the decoder spans the horizon.
Evidence
5-fold: no covariates 88.4, encoder only 93.1, decoder only 63.9, both 61.6. Fold 4 alone: 88.4, 90.1, 59.8, 59.0.

A learned bridge replaces the repeated-mean trend startTested, small effect

Reference
The trend stream starts as the mean of the encoder input repeated over the horizon, plus the decomposed label trend over the label span.
This model
The trend stream starts at zero and receives a bridge: the sum over j of gj[h] times the mean of the last wj values of y, with w in {1, 3, 12, 48}. The gain g is learned per horizon step, starts at 0, and is scaled by 10 internally so the optimiser can move it in time.
Why
Repeating a mean over 168 steps hard-codes persistence, and the mean-removed seasonal path cannot cancel it. My first version of this idea (a short y label in the decoder) trained to RMSE about 80 and was abandoned. The bridge starts exactly y-free and lets the data decide how far recent y reaches. With four scalars as input it cannot memorise a window.
Evidence
A 6-seed mean of 63.1 against 64.5 without, a gain of about 1.4 ± 1.0 that is not significant. The gain appears at long horizons (h above 48: 59.7 against 63.0). In the first hours persistence (RMSE 19.6 at h = 0) still beats the model (about 68). An explicit blend gate and position channels were tried and rejected (section 10).

Cross-correlation on a shared, absolute timelineNot ablated

Reference
Queries, keys and values are padded or truncated at the end to a common length, so position 0 lines up with position 0.
This model
Encoder times [o−L, o) and decoder times [o−LB, o+H) are placed on one timeline of length L+H, zero-padded, and the correlation is divided by the number of overlapping steps.
Why
A lag of τ should mean τ real hours between a decoder position and an encoder position. Here the encoder spans 336 steps and the decoder 216, so aligning position 0 would make the same τ mean different real offsets.
Evidence
Reasoning only. I have no measurement showing it improves RMSE.

Delay aggregation as an FFT convolutionEquivalence tested

Reference
A loop over K rolled copies of the values (a gather for each delay).
This model
The K softmax weights are scattered into a kernel ω of length T, and the output is irfft(rfft(V) · rfft(ω)).
Why
The result is identical, but the gather version materialised B × K × d × T numbers and ran out of GPU memory when several runs shared the card. The convolution needs B × d × T.
Evidence
A self-test against a brute-force O(T²) computation matches to 1e-4. No accuracy change was expected, and none was measured.

Per-example delays that look backwards, with a learned temperatureNot ablated

Reference
In training the delays are chosen from the correlation averaged over the batch and shared by every example. At inference they are chosen per example. Values are shifted by −τ, so the output at t reads vt+τ. The softmax over the top-K scores has no temperature.
This model
Delays are chosen per example in training and inference. The output is zt = sum of wj vt−τj, so it reads earlier steps, as in the manual's Equation 6. The softmax divides by a learned temperature.
Why
Training and test follow one rule, and each window picks its own lags. Information flows from earlier hours to later ones, which matches a state that depends on earlier conditions. The temperature lets the model decide how sharply to favour the best delays.
Evidence
The self-test checks the direction convention. None of the three choices was ablated.

Replicate-padded convolution embedding, no calendar embeddingNot ablated

Reference
A convolution of width 3 with circular padding, plus a fixed positional embedding and a learned calendar embedding.
This model
A convolution of width 3 with replicate padding and no positional or calendar embedding. Config T adds hour-of-day as sine and cosine channels computed from the index modulo 24.
Why
Circular padding lets the last hours of a window bleed into the first. There are no timestamps, only an integer index, and hour-of-day can be recovered from it because the 24-step cycle is visible in the covariates.
Evidence
The padding mode was not ablated. Hour-of-day was neutral: per-seed mean 62.7 against 61.6, ensemble 58.8 against 58.6. It stays out of the base model and is in 3 of the 12 members for diversity.

Log-scale output with an exp link, trained on physical MSEAblated

Reference
A linear output on the standardised target, with MSE in that space.
This model
The network outputs z on the standardised log(1 + y) scale, the forecast is expm1(z × sd + mu), and the loss is MSE in physical units, masked on interpolated rows.
Why
y is non-negative and heavy-tailed. The log scale makes covariate effects multiplicative and keeps forecasts at or above zero. A loss in physical units is exactly the ranked RMSE, so large values get the weight the leaderboard gives them.
Evidence
With 3 seeds each on the final configuration: 61.6 against 69.7 per seed, and 58.6 against 65.6 for the ensembles.

Zero-initialised output headNot ablated

Reference
Default initialisation.
This model
The output weights and bias start at zero, so the model begins by predicting expm1(mu), the typical level.
Why
Training starts near a sensible answer and does not spend epochs learning the output scale.
Evidence
Reasoning only.

A small networkCompared

Reference
The published settings, as I remember them, use width 512 with 2 encoder layers and a wide feed-forward layer.
This model
Width 32, 4 heads, 1 encoder layer, 1 decoder layer, feed-forward width 64, dropout 0.1. About 38,000 parameters per member.
Why
There are only about 260 independent weeks. Training RMSE of 35 to 45 against held-out 63 shows overfitting even at this size. Parameters are almost free in the score (α is about 1e-9) but not in generalisation.
Evidence
Width 64 with 2 encoder layers: 63.4 against 61.6 per seed, ensemble 59.6 against 58.6 (3 seeds each). Width 16: 63.3 (one seed). The larger one is config B in the ensemble, for diversity.

Minor simplificationsNot ablated

Reference
The trend is projected with a convolution, and the feed-forward block uses 1 × 1 convolutions with a wider hidden layer.
This model
The trend is projected with a linear layer from d to 1. The feed-forward block is linear, GELU, dropout, linear, with hidden width 2d.
Why
Fewer parameters and simpler code for the same job.
Evidence
Not tested separately.

What stays as in the paper

  • Progressive series decomposition after every sub-layer, with a centred moving average of kernel 25 and edge replication.
  • The accumulated trend stream in the decoder, and the seasonal normalisation (LayerNorm followed by removing the mean over time).
  • Auto-correlation by FFT with the top K = ⌊3 ln T⌋ delays, followed by a softmax.
  • The encoder-decoder shape with a label length of 48.

7Training

AdamW with learning rate 1e-3 on a one-cycle schedule, weight decay 1e-4, batch 128, dropout 0.1, gradient clipping at 1, and 30 epochs on windows at stride 4. An "epoch" is one pass over that window set. In stride-1 terms 30 is about 7.5 epochs. I declare 30, the larger number.

The final ensemble has four configurations:

  • A: d 32, 1 encoder layer, kernel 25.
  • T: A plus hour-of-day marks.
  • B: d 64, 2 encoder layers.
  • K: A with decomposition kernel 13.

Each has 3 seeds, so 12 members. Mixing configurations helps: 6 seeds of one configuration reach 58.7, 12 members across four configurations reach 57.0, and all 18 bridge-based models reach 57.4. An early 6-member subset reached 56.9, but I chose it after seeing results, so I do not use it.

8Results

  • Per-fold CV RMSE of the 12-member ensemble: 67.5, 51.7, 56.5, 55.3 and 53.5, pooled 57.0. Fold 0, the first year, is consistently the hardest.
  • Calibration of the out-of-fold predictions: bias −0.7 and regression slope 0.965. A linear post-correction made it worse (57.6), so none is applied.
  • Per-block RMSE over 257 held-out blocks: median 43.6, mean 51.0, 10th and 90th percentiles 27 and 88.5.
  • Decomposition off: 66.7 → 62.9 for single-seed mean and ensemble, against 61.6 → 58.6 with it.

9Validation protocol

Frozen before the architecture work.

  • Leave-one-year-out, five folds. Training windows must avoid the held-out cycle plus or minus 96 steps, including their contexts. This is asserted in af_cv.run.
  • Test-mimicking. For an origin o, y is visible only in [o−L, o) and covariates in [o−L, o+H). Trailing windows end at t, so nothing beyond the horizon is used (see the timeline in section 5).
  • The standardisation of y is fitted on the training targets of each fold. Scoring uses non-overlapping 168-blocks.
  • The hidden block is the final block of the last year. Fold 4, which trains on years 1 to 4 and tests year 5, is its direct analogue.

10Things that failed or were bugs

  • The first y-free experiments scored about 100 because of my own bug. It zeroed the whole accumulated trend whenever y was off (tr * 0). With no trend branch, the mean-removed seasonal path cannot output a level. It is fixed, with a regression test, and every y-free number from before the fix was discarded.
  • Position-in-horizon channels hurt (64.8). A learned blend-with-persistence gate fixed the first hours (RMSE at h = 0 fell from 71 to 25) but cost more elsewhere. The ensemble scored 59.6 to 59.7 against 58.6, so it is not used.
  • Input noise, hour-of-day marks, a larger model, 60 epochs and kernel 13 were none clearly better than the base configuration, within seed noise of about ±1.9.
